I was not an athletic kid. Not modestly unathletic — genuinely, comprehensively out of it. Last picked in school, out of breath on stairs, the person who treated "let's play football" as a threat. For most of my life, "runner" was a category of person I understood myself not to be, the way some people aren't morning people.

Then, a few years ago, I went for a run. It went badly.

## The first attempt

I made it about a kilometre before I had to stop, and the stopping wasn't dramatic — no collapse, no heroic struggle. My legs were fine. My lungs simply refused. I walked home genuinely embarrassed, and the embarrassment was the interesting part: I was embarrassed in front of nobody, on an empty road, at six in the morning.

That's the first thing worth saying to anyone in the same position. **The barrier is almost never physical at the start.** A healthy body with no training can shuffle for twenty minutes. What stops you is the story you're carrying about what kind of person you are, and the certainty that everyone can see you failing at something children do for fun.

What got me past it was lowering the bar until it was insultingly easy. Not "run 5k". Not even "run". The goal became: **leave the house in running shoes, four times a week.** How far I went was explicitly not the metric. Some days that meant thirty minutes of running. Plenty of days it meant walking for fifteen minutes and coming home.

It sounds like a cop-out. It's the only reason I'm still running today.

## What actually built the distance

Nothing I did was clever. Every bit of it is in every beginner's guide ever written, which is itself the lesson — this isn't a domain with secrets, it's a domain with patience.

**Run-walk intervals.** Run one minute, walk two. Then run two, walk two. Then three and one. Over a couple of months, the walking intervals shrank until they weren't there. Doing this from the start would have saved me weeks of the try-hard-then-quit cycle, because run-walk lets you accumulate *time on feet* — which is what actually builds aerobic base — without wrecking yourself.

**Running far slower than felt right.** This was the hardest change and the most important one. Almost everyone who starts running goes too fast, because the pace that feels like "running" is closer to a tempo effort than an easy one. Slowing down to a pace where I could hold a conversation felt like cheating. Then the distance started climbing on its own, and I understood: easy pace isn't a lesser version of hard running, it's a different training stimulus, and it's the one that builds endurance.

**Adding distance slowly, and taking rest seriously.** Roughly ten percent a week, with a lighter week every fourth. The one time I ignored this and jumped my long run because I felt great, I got shin splints and lost a month. Ambition is the injury mechanism.

**Getting shoes that fit my feet.** Not expensive ones — fitted ones. Knee pain I'd assumed was "just what running does to you" mostly disappeared.

**Strength work, eventually.** Squats, lunges, calf raises, planks. Twice a week. I resisted this for a long time on the grounds that I wanted to run, not lift, which is a bit like refusing to write tests because you'd rather ship features. The legs that carry you through hour three are built in a room, not on a road.

The distance came in unglamorous increments: 3k became 5k, 5k became a 10k that took two months to feel comfortable, 10k became 15k. There was no breakthrough day. There was just a Sunday, maybe a year in, when I finished a long run and realised I'd covered a distance that would have been unimaginable to the person who couldn't finish that first kilometre.

## The trails changed everything

Road running kept me fit. **Trail running is what made me love it.**

The first trail run I did was slower than my slowest road run and twice as hard. Loose rock, roots, a climb that reduced me to a walk within four minutes. I finished it filthy and exhausted and immediately wanted to go back.

Trails are different in ways I didn't expect:

**You stop thinking about pace.** On a road, you're chasing a number and constantly negotiating with it. On a trail, pace is meaningless — a technical descent and a fire road aren't comparable, so the metric quietly becomes *effort*, which is the metric that was always more honest.

**Your attention has somewhere to go.** Trail running demands you look at the ground. Where's the next foot landing, is that root wet, can you take that line. There's no spare capacity for ruminating about a production incident. It's the closest thing to enforced presence I've found, and it's why a bad-mood run on trails works better than any amount of trying to relax.

**Walking is allowed.** Everyone walks the steep climbs, including people much faster than me. Removing the shame from walking removed the last psychological barrier to going further, because now the long run isn't a test you can fail — it's just a day out.

**The variety keeps the legs honest.** Uneven ground recruits stabilising muscles that road running never touches. My ankles are more robust than they've ever been.

The 25k came out of this. Not as a goal I trained for from the front, but as the natural next thing after a 15k that had felt fine and a 20k that had felt survivable. It took most of a morning. I walked all the steep climbs, ate more than I thought I'd need, and finished slower than I started — and I finished, which was the only thing I'd wanted to know.

## Runner's high is real, and it isn't what I expected

I assumed the "high" was marketing. Something magazine runners said to justify the hours.

It's real. But it's not euphoria — or at least, that's not the part that keeps me coming back.

What actually happens, somewhere past the forty-minute mark on a long easy run, is that the effort stops requiring a decision. The negotiation in your head goes quiet. Your breathing, your footfall and your thinking settle into the same rhythm, and you notice you've covered two kilometres without deciding to. There's a lightness to it, a slight sense that you could keep going indefinitely, and — the strangest part — an unearned goodwill toward everything and everyone.

The physiological story involves endorphins and endocannabinoids, and researchers are still arguing about the exact mix. I can't tell you what's happening in my bloodstream. I can tell you it lands most reliably on long, slow, easy runs, and almost never when I'm pushing hard. Chasing it directly doesn't work. It arrives when you stop performing and just move.

And the version of it that comes *after* the run is arguably better: two or three hours of calm competence, where problems that felt tangled in the morning have visible edges. I've solved more work problems on trails than at my desk, and I don't think that's a coincidence. It's the same reason the shower works, extended over three hours.

## What running gave me that isn't fitness

The physical stuff is real and worth stating plainly: my resting heart rate dropped, I sleep properly, my back stopped complaining about desk work, and I have an aerobic base that makes ordinary life — stairs, flights, long days — noticeably easier. The mental health effect is not subtle either; a run is the most reliable intervention I have for a bad week.

But the thing I actually value most is what it did to my sense of what's possible.

I spent two decades certain that I was not an athletic person. That was, it turns out, entirely a claim about my history and not at all a claim about my capacity. I changed it by doing something small four times a week for long enough that it stopped being a project and started being a fact about my life.

That generalises uncomfortably well. Nearly everything I've believed I "wasn't the kind of person to do" has turned out to be a training problem wearing an identity costume. Learning Go was a training problem. Writing publicly was a training problem. The pattern is always the same: make the first repetition small enough to be embarrassing, do it more often than feels meaningful, and let the compounding do work your motivation can't.

## If you're where I was

Some things I'd tell the version of me standing at the end of that first kilometre:

- **Go slower than feels legitimate.** If you can't talk, you're going too fast. That's not encouragement, it's the actual training principle.
- **Walk. Walking is part of running,** not an admission of failure.
- **Commit to frequency, not distance.** Four short runs a week beats one heroic one, every time.
- **Take the rest days seriously.** You get fitter while recovering, not while running.
- **Get properly fitted shoes** before you buy anything else.
- **Find a trail.** Even a scrappy one. The moment running stops being about the numbers, it stops being a chore.
- **Expect the first three weeks to be unpleasant.** Somewhere in week four or five, the shift happens. Almost everyone quits before it.

I'm not fast. I've never been fast, and at this point I've made peace with the fact that I probably won't be. But I can spend a morning moving through the hills under my own power and come home happy, which is a thing the last-picked kid would not have believed was available to him.

It was available the whole time. It just needed a very slow first kilometre.
